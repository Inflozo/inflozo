WHAT THE EDITOR DRAWS · the behaviour does not run while editing
§2 says behaviours do not run while editing, and this is the design that tests it. A section whose resting state hides five of six items cannot be edited if the only way to reach item four is to click a tab that has been disabled. So while editing, the panel draws whichever item is selected in the sidebar's item list — selecting the fourth item shows the fourth panel, and the tab row updates to match. It is a preview, not a saved value: a published page always opens on the first item, and there is no “default tab” control, because the first item is the first item. The tab row itself is not clickable while editing — clicking a tab selects the tab for editing its label, as every other piece of text in the section behaves. That is the one difference between the editing and published states, and the editor says it in a line under the item list. Flagged as mine: the whole editing model, the refusal of a default-tab control, and the click-to-edit behaviour of the row.

With no picture the panel is one 620 px measure at the left, the item title steps 28 → 24 px, and the panel's height is the tallest item's text plus its link — which is where the fixed-height rule earns its place: without it, a two-line item after a five-line one would lift the whole page. A site whose items differ by more than about 120 px of text is advised toward 6 Rows, where every item is visible at once and nothing has to be reserved.

SIX TABS IS THE CEILING
Six tab labels at 15 px with 28 px between them is about 900 px of the 1,296 available, which leaves room for the longest label a site will write. At seven items the row wraps, and a wrapped tab row is two rows of tabs — which is a menu. So seven items is where the editor names 6 Rows or 4 Cards, and the twelve-item category cap is never reached on this design. The tabLabel field's 20-character cap is what keeps six workable, and where a label is absent the tab reads the item's title clipped to 20 characters with the clip disclosed. Flagged as mine: the six-tab ceiling, the 900 px arithmetic, and both named destinations.

1 · The row is a role="tablist" of <button role="tab">, each with aria-selected and aria-controls. Buttons, not links: the panel is on this page and nothing navigates. The tablist has an aria-label naming the section's own title, so a reader meeting it out of context knows what is being switched. 2 · Roving tabindex: one tab stop for the whole row, arrow keys between tabs, Home and End to the ends. Activation follows the arrow keys — the panel changes as the reader moves, which is the right choice here because the panels are cheap, local content rather than pages. No auto-rotation, ever, and no aria-live: the reader caused the change. 3 · The panel is a role="tabpanel" labelled by its tab, tabindex="0" so it can be reached from the row, and it contains the item's h3. The h3 repeats the tab's label deliberately — a panel whose heading is missing because the tab said it already leaves a heading gap in the outline, and the repetition costs a reader one short line. 4 · The hidden panels are hidden, not off-screen — five items are not in the accessibility tree and their pictures are not fetched until first shown. That is the honest cost of this design and the reason it is not the category's default: a reader who does not touch the tabs sees one feature out of six, and a site whose six features all matter has 4 Cards or 6 Rows. In-page search finds only the open panel; the editor says so. Contrast · active tab 12.1:1 light / 11.9:1 dark · resting tab 5.4:1 / 6.1:1 · underline 4.8:1 / 4.9:1, non-text · item title 12.1:1 / 11.9:1 · body 5.4:1 / 6.1:1. The active state is carried by weight and colour as well as the underline, so it does not depend on one 2 px line.
