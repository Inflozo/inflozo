WHICH ITEM IS THE SPOTLIGHT
The first item, always, and there is no control for it. 9 Bento made the same decision and this design inherits it: the spotlight is a position in the item list, so a user changes it by dragging an item to the top, which is a thing they already know how to do. The frames here spotlight Full archive rather than the weekly letter, which means the authored order for this design differs from the rest of the category's — the second item has been dragged to the front. That is the point of drawing it: the section's own copy says the archive is what people stay for, and this is the design that can say so structurally. §7·1's content parity holds across this design's frames; the order differs from other designs' by a stated user action. Flagged as mine: first-item-as-spotlight, the absence of a control, and re-ordering the category's items on this design.

Field notes
1 · One list, six items, and the spotlight is the first of them. The spotlight and the entries are the same <ul> — the first <li> is simply drawn larger. Two lists, or a list plus a figure, would tell a reader there are two kinds of thing here, and there is one kind at two sizes. 2 · The picture is decorative by default and its alt is authorable, as everywhere in A5. It is inside the first <li> and before the title in the DOM at both Picture side values — the visual side is row-reverse, never a reorder, as on 7 and 9. 3 · The entries are h3s at 17 px, and the spotlight's title is an h3 at 34 px. Same level, different size — the emphasis is visual, and A4·16's rule that visual size is not heading level applies in both directions. Nothing here is aria-current or “featured”: the spotlight is the first item, which the reading order already says. At The rest Hidden the other five items are not rendered at all — not hidden, not display:none, absent — and the list is a list of one. They stay in the content so the site can switch designs, and the editor says how many are held back. One tab stop in the section; no motion at any value.
