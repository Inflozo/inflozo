PART C · THE POST-BODY TRIO

What happens inside a blog post.

Three surfaces that are really one: the cards a Ghost author inserts while writing, the section that renders their words, and the paywall that interrupts them. None of the three existed in the product before this pass.

Every control in C.1 is derived from the markup Ghost's {{content}} helper actually emits, and from the card CSS Ghost ships as cards.min.css. Where Ghost gives the setting to the post author, no control is offered — the designer styles the variants the author can produce instead.
C.1 · THE MODULE'S HOME

Every card the editor can insert

Twenty-two cards, grouped by how much of them a theme can actually reach. Ghost ships its own CSS for thirteen of them; taking one over adds it to the theme's card_assets.exclude list, which is what the Yours badge means in practice. The rest inherit the post-content section's typography and have little or nothing of their own to set.

Notes. The grouping is the honest version of a card library: thirteen cards have Ghost-authored CSS a theme can exclude and replace, six emit ordinary elements that simply inherit the article's typography, and three never reach a web page at all. Grouping them this way stops the module promising design work it cannot do — and it is also the order a developer needs, since only the first group touches card_assets.

The four hundred domains that refuse to move

Every October, a few hundred people renew an address they never expected anyone to visit. This is what they have in common, and what happens the year they stop.
RM
Rosa Menendez 9 min read · Members
A 2004 capture of the oldest address on the list, still renewed by the same person.
T he domains are the tell. Fifteen years on the same address, renewed every October by someone who never expected an audience and got one anyway. I have been keeping a list since 2019, and it has never once grown.
What they share is not taste. Some are beautiful and most are not. It is a refusal to move — off the address, off the software, off the habit — which turns out to be the rarest thing on the web.
💡 If you are looking for the list itself, it is at the end of this issue — and every link on it was checked the week this went out.

Who is actually paying

Three of the four hundred are paid for by estates. Two are paid for by universities that forgot they were paying. The rest are individuals, and their renewal dates cluster in October for a reason nobody has explained to my satisfaction.
A site that outlives three platform migrations is not nostalgic. It is just paid for. Kicks Condor, 2019
I asked eleven of them why October. The answers divided cleanly:
·Four said the date meant nothing at all, and were mildly annoyed to be asked.
·Three said it was simply when they had money, which is the honest version of the same answer.
·Two said October was when their first host used to bill them. That host closed in 2013.

The renewal arithmetic

A domain costs about twelve dollars a year. Hosting a static site costs nothing at three of the four common hosts. The whole obligation, then, is one dollar a month and one afternoon a decade.

The counting itself is unglamorous. I run one request a month against each address and record what comes back, which is fourteen lines of shell and a text file:
#!/usr/bin/env bash while read -r host; do code=$(curl -sS -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}' \ --max-time 10 "https://$host/" || echo 000) printf '%s\t%s\t%s\n' "$(date -I)" "$host" "$code" done < hosts.txt >> log.tsv
Anything that answers 200 twice in a row goes on the list. Anything that answers 000 for three months comes off it, and I check by hand before it does.
Why not just use the Internet Archive?
Because the Archive tells you a page existed, not that someone is still paying for it. Those are different questions, and only the second one predicts next October.

Notes. The fixture has to do two jobs that pull against each other: exercise every treatment, and read as something a person wrote. The compromise is sequencing — cards arrive where an article would naturally want them (a callout after the opening claim, a bookmark after a citation, the gallery under a sentence that promises pictures, the file card in the section about the spreadsheet) rather than in a checklist. Three cards appear only in their alternate states elsewhere in this document, because two of one card in a single article would look like a bug: the toggle here is closed, the blockquote here is standard, the header card is not present at all — it belongs at the top of a post, where this fixture already has a real title.
