Image Split

A photograph on one half, the accordion on the other, both top-aligned, on a 32 px gutter. The only design in A9 that reads image — the other fourteen keep the field — and the design a site picks when the FAQ sits on a page that would otherwise be four hundred words of type.

A9's own problem arrives with it: the rows grow when a reader opens one and the photograph does not. Neither column stretches to match the other, and the shorter one simply ends — which is why both are top-aligned and why there is no value in this design that centres them.

The crop is named, not computed — A8·8's rule, carried. Portrait 4:5 at 632 is 790 px, Square is 632, Landscape 3:2 is 421. The image is object-fit: cover centred on its own centre with no focal-point control, and the editor says a site that needs a different focal point should crop the file. Which crop to pick is a question about the rows, and the panel says so: six closed rows at Large 20 are about 640 px, so Square is the crop that comes closest to matching them and Portrait is the one that leaves the most white space beside the rows. Landscape is for a site whose rows are long and whose photograph is a scene rather than a subject. There is no scrim and nothing is laid over the photograph, at any value: no text on the image, no caption inside it, no rounded card floating on it. 15 Overlap's scrim rules in A8 exist because text sat on a band; nothing sits on this one. The image has the pack radius and no shadow. It is a plane on the ground beside another plane on the ground, and a shadow on one of two equal halves says one of them is nearer.

ACCESSIBILITY · THE ONE IMAGE IN A9 The photograph is an <img> with real alt text from imageAlt, requested by the editor, alt="" where none is authored, never a filename and never a CSS background. It is the only image in the whole category, which is why the field list carries imageAlt at all. The two halves are a flex row, not two landmarks — no <aside>, no <figure> on a live page, no roles. One <h2>, one <ul>, <h3> + full-width <summary> per row, the answer the <details>’s own content — no role="region" and no hidden attribute. Image side changes DOM order and is disclosed as doing so. At Left the photograph is read before the questions; at Right, after them. Neither is wrong and the control's help line says which is which, because a reader on a screen reader meets the image where the markup puts it. The image is not a link, has no hover state, and does not move — no zoom, no parallax, no lightbox. A14 Galleries owns anything a reader can open a photograph into. Contrast: question and answer 15.8:1 light / 15.1:1 dark, marker 5.6:1 / 6.2:1, link underline 3.3:1 / 6.3:1 — the photograph carries no text, so it has no contrast requirement. In forced colours the image is kept and the striped placeholder becomes a bordered box; the two halves stack, because a system-coloured page has no plane to divide. Print: the image is kept above the rows in one column and every row prints open.

Questions and answers edit inline on canvas, selected where they are drawn rather than only in the repeater. The answer raises the marks toolbar on selection with bold · italic · link · inline code and no underline — an underline inside prose is a link that is not one. Recorded as a PRD delta. Nothing in A9 is Ghost-owned, so nothing here says “Edit in Ghost” — every word on the page is the site’s own. The link opens the Ghost-aware Link Picker and takes an optional P0·2 icon before or after its label, off by default. Questions are the P0·3 item list: Add question arrives with content, Remove is never disabled, drag to reorder, per-item content only. The photograph carries Image focus — the shared control, defined once in P0·9 and not restated here, in the Image Picker popover under the photograph, never a hidden field. Both axes are drawn, as P0·9 defines them, and this design narrows neither: a wide photograph in a 4:5 crop loses its sides, so which side survives is a choice worth having. Ghost never sees this — focus is a hint the compiler resolves into the crop the theme ships with, which is why it is a control and not a data binding, declares no behaviour module, and is not lost with JavaScript off. The design’s old refusal was of a free focal point — a draggable point or a percentage, which P0·9 refuses library-wide — and named values are not one; Crop still names the three ratios.

One control name means one set of valuesThe panel’s Image focus line no longer enumerates the control’s values. It names the control, names the slot it sits under here — the section’s image, in its Image Picker popover — and points at P0·9, where the control is defined once for the library. The side-to-side axis added by the owner’s ruling of 3 September 2026 is in force here by reference rather than by restatement, and the panel says both axes are drawn without writing their values out. The Crop row’s help line stops asserting a centre of its own and points at focus for it. Nothing drawn changed: the three named crops, the two divisions, the two sides and every geometry on this frame stand as they were.
A design may offer fewer choices on a shared control and must say whyNo subject — and that is stated rather than left silent. This design narrows neither axis, so there is no constraint to justify. Its standing refusal is of a free focal point — a draggable point or a percentage — which P0·9 refuses library-wide, and which is not a narrowing of the named values.
A control that could never do anything here is not drawn, and the panel says whyNo subject on this design. All three crops cut a real file — Portrait 4:5 is 790 px at 632, Square 632, Landscape 3:2 421, and the stack below 1081 forces 3:2 — so focus always has work to do. Nothing is greyed, nothing withheld, and no note stands in a control’s place.
Ghost never sees this: focus is a compiler hint, not a data bindingWritten in, once, in the Editing group. Focus adds no field, reads nothing from Ghost, sends nothing to it, declares no behaviour module and is not lost with JavaScript off. The Data group’s statement — nothing here comes from Ghost — is unaffected and unedited.
