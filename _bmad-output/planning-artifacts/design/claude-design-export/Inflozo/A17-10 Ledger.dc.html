Ledger

Title, tag and date on one hairline-divided row, in two columns, eight or twelve to a screen. The only table archetype in A17 — and the design that takes the category's premise literally: an archive is a list of things published on dates, and a reader scanning one wants the date, not the photograph.

It is the counterweight to 9 Big Type, which suggests this design from about eight posts. Where that one fails by putting too many things at one size, this one is built for exactly that — the row is a unit of scanning rather than of composition.

It is also one of only two designs that stay two-across on a phone. Every image-bearing design collapses to one column at 767 because a 163 px image is a thumbnail; a title and a date have no such floor, so this design keeps its columns where the others give theirs up.

Eight rows in two 636 columns, four rows deep. That density is the design: a reader who wants to see what a publication has been doing sees eight titles without scrolling, where 1 Three Up needs the same height for two.
The date is right-aligned on tabular numerals, and it is the one place in A17 that asks for a font feature. font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums makes “12 Mar 2026” and “5 Mar 2026” end on the same pixel, so the column's right edge is a line rather than a fringe. ⚑ A finding: not every pack's body font has tabular figures, and where it does not the declaration is ignored and the edge goes slightly ragged. It degrades acceptably — but it is a pack requirement A17 has no way to state.
The date is abbreviated to “12 Mar 2026”, not the category's “12 March 2026”. A full month name makes the date column as wide as its longest month and steals it from the title at every row. The <time datetime> attribute still carries the full ISO date, so nothing is lost to a machine — only to the eye, deliberately. ⚑ Mine, and the only place in A17 where a date is abbreviated.
Measured, the first row is 102 px and the other three are 78 — because post 2's 69-character title is the only one that wraps to two lines at this measure, and a row is as tall as its tallest cell. Post 4 has no tag, and its box still matches post 3's beside it: the missing tag shows as space at the foot of the row, not as a shorter row. That is the same stretch behaviour as every grid in A17 — what differs is that here the variation is one line rather than a whole card, so the hairlines stay evenly spaced enough to scan.

Date Lead gives the date a fixed 96 px column and makes it the thing being scanned. It is the setting for a chronological archive, where a reader is looking for “what came out in February”; Date Right is for a reader looking for a subject. The same content, two different questions. The DOM order does not change at either setting — the title is always first — so the date moving is visual only. Rows Open removes the hairlines and tightens the row padding from 16 to 10, which is denser still. It requires Tag Off to stay readable: without a rule, a two-line row and a one-line row next to each other stop having a clear boundary. The panel discloses the pairing rather than forcing it — “Open rows read best with the tag off.” At Date Lead with a missing tag nothing shifts, because the date column's width is fixed rather than derived from content. That is the one advantage Date Lead has over Date Right, where the date column is as wide as its widest date.

THREE · 416 · THE DATE DROPS UNDER THE TITLE — NO ROOM FOR A RIGHT-HAND COLUMN
⚑ The reading-order problem, and it is this design's real weakness. Two columns of rows read left-to-right then down, because that is DOM order in a wrapping list — but a ledger of dates invites the eye to read down the left column first, and a reader who does that gets 12 Mar, 5 Mar, 26 Feb, 18 Feb and concludes the second column is a different list. Newspapers solve this with a rule between columns; I have not, because a vertical rule at every row is a table border and A17 refuses heavy borders. What the design does instead is accept it and stay shallow. At eight posts the two columns are four rows deep and the misreading costs a glance; at twenty-four it would cost real confusion. Columns One is the honest setting for a long archive and the panel says so from about sixteen posts: “Long lists read better in one column.” At Columns Three the date moves under the title — a 416 row cannot hold a title and a right-aligned date without the title breaking every time. It is the one setting where this design's row stops being a row and becomes a small stack, which is why Three is available but is not the design's intent.

SIX CONTROLS, AND EVERY ONE OF THEM IS ABOUT DENSITYThis design already governed its tag, so the category-wide Tag row changes nothing here. Padding retired into Vertical spacing; “View all” link added.With photograph is not offered ⚑ — there is no author at any setting, which is also why the Meta enum never reached this design. Columns, Rows, Date, Tag, Title and Padding — all six change how much fits on a screen. No other design in A17 has a control list this coherent, and the reason is that density is the only variable a table has. There is no image to place, no ratio to pick, no excerpt to clamp; what remains is how many rows a reader sees at once. ⚑ Measured, Title Small 17 against Standard 19 saves only about 4% of a row's height — a 78 px row becomes 75 — because the row is mostly padding and tag, not title. So Title is the weakest density control here, and the honest ranking is the opposite of the obvious one: Tag Off removes 20 px a row and Rows Open 13 more, taking a 78 px row to 45 — a 42% gain against Title Small's 4%. All three together reach 42 px, a 46% gain, and Title Small is worth having only once the other two are already on. The panel's note is corrected to say so, because a user reaching for density needs to know which lever actually moves. Date Off exists and it is the strangest value in this design, because a ledger without dates is a list of titles. It is here for the by-tag case: a section showing everything tagged Interviews, where the dates are irrelevant and the titles are the point. Disclosed as a value rather than argued for. Cut: any hover treatment beyond the category's underline. A table invites a row highlight — a fill on hover, the way an email client does — and A17-0 refuses it: a fill on a bare row needs a surface token the row does not have, and at Rows Open there is no row boundary for a fill to respect. The title's accent underline is the whole hover state, as everywhere in the category.

One column keeps the row intact — title left, date right — and is the better setting on a phone for anything over about ten posts. Two columns at 157 px each is dense but every title breaks to three lines.

At two columns on a phone the row stops being a row: the date drops under the title and the tag is dropped entirely. This design has more hairlines than any other in A17 — one per row, so eight at Count 8 and twenty-four at Count 24 — which makes it the design where the hairline token's dark value matters most. Measured, the two hairlines are the same proportional step from their own grounds: #E7E2DB sits 9.1 lightness points below #FBF9F5, and #332E27 sits 9.8 points above #171511. Dark needs no special step here — the symmetric one already works, which is worth stating because the temptation in a design with this many rules is to strengthen them and end up with a grid of boxes. A17-0 sets the token and 3 Four Up drew it for two rows; here it is drawn for eight, which is the real test of whether one value serves both modes.
Nothing else in this design changes in dark — no images, no plates, no planes, no scrims, and the tabular figures are a font feature rather than a colour. Four tokens and a border, in both modes.
THE WRITTEN SPEC · ALL TEN FIELDS
1 · Descriptor. Title, tag and abbreviated date on one hairline-divided row, two columns across, rows as tall as their contents — the archive as a table rather than a grid. 2 · Structural descriptor. table · none · page · many · none · hairline row rhythm
Unique on the archetype slot — the only table in A17. 3 · Archetype. table. Its ladder reduces columns and sheds fields rather than narrowing cells, which is why it can keep two columns at 390 where every grid-of-N design cannot. 4 · Responsive rule. 1440 two 636 rows, 24 gutter, row padding 16, title 19, date “12 Mar 2026” right-aligned on tabular numerals. 834 two at 365, padding 14, title 18, the year drops — “12 Mar”. ≤ 767 — the departure from the category floor: two columns are kept at 157 each, but the row stops being a row — the date moves under the title, the tag is dropped, and both Date Right and Date Lead resolve to “under the title”, so that control silently stops applying. At Columns One the row survives intact at every width, which is why the panel names it for long archives. 5 · Content fields. Section: the six, all optional, link as a pair. Query: all four. Post: title, url, published_at, primary_tag at Tag On. Four fields, the fewest of any design in A17. Draws no feature_image, no custom_excerpt, no primary_author and no reading_time at any setting. Reads featured, never draws it; visibility read, never drawn. 6 · Controls. Columns One · Two · Three. Rows Divided · Open. Date Right · Lead · Off. Tag On · Off. Title Standard · Small. “View all” link Matches the query · Custom. Six, plus the universal trio (Background role · Vertical spacing · Top divider) and the Data group, both outside this list. Five of the six are density controls, which no other design in A17 can say. No Meta enum and no With photograph — there is no author at any setting.
7 · Data. Ghost's #get "posts" helper per the shared Data group. Designed for 8 and 12; correct at 2 to 30 — the widest working range in A17, because a table gets better with more rows rather than worse. 0: section does not render; in the editor, three outlined ruled rows and the query line. 1: one row with a rule above it, which reads as a fragment of a table rather than a composition — this design's weakest single-post case, and it is disclosed rather than special-cased. Odd counts: the last row sits alone in the left column with no rule opposite it, which reads correctly. From about sixteen the panel suggests Columns One. More than the count: not drawn; A34 below. 8 · Empty state. No tag → the row is one line instead of two, and the rows are deliberately not equalised — a table's rows are as tall as their contents, and the hairline is what keeps the unevenness from reading as an error. No tag on any post → the design is titles and dates, which is its most reduced and arguably best form. A missing feature image is not an empty state; the field is never read. Section fields absent → not drawn. 9 · Behaviour module. None; core assumed. No-JS degradation: pixel-identical with JavaScript off — text, a border, a link and a font feature. Not declared: filter-strip, which would be the obvious addition to a table and which 15 Filtered owns; and no sort control of any kind. A table invites sorting by column, and A17's Order is Newest or Oldest first with no third value — a “sort by title” would need JavaScript and no module covers it. A finding, not a gap: Ghost can order by title server-side, so the honest fix is a third Order value rather than a client-side sort. 10 · Accessibility. <section aria-labelledby> → <h2>; no accessible name at Head None. ⚑ It is a <ul> of <li>, not a <table>, despite the archetype's name. There are no column headers, no row headers and no cell relationships to announce — it is a list that looks like a table, and marking it up as one would promise a screen-reader user navigation that does not exist. The archetype names its responsive behaviour, not its markup. Title an <h3> in the row's <a>; tag and date <p>s inside it. The abbreviated date is visual only — <time datetime="2026-03-12"> carries the full date at every width, including the phone where the year is not drawn. Hairlines are decorative border-tops, never <hr>s. A6's ring on the whole row. Contrast: title #232019 on #FBF9F5 at 15.46:1 and muted #6B6459 at 5.56:1 light; #F2EDE4 at 15.64:1 and #A79E8F at 6.89:1 dark — the same four values as 9 Big Type, since both designs are text on the page ground.
