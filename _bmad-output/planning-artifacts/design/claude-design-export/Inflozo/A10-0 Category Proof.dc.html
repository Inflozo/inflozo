A10 STATS AND NUMBERS · CATEGORY ARTEFACTS · READ FIRST · PAPER PACK

Fifteen designs, one opt-in module, and nothing that needs it

A9's problem was that ten of fifteen designs withheld their own content. A10 has the opposite problem: every design shows everything it has, immediately, and there is nothing for a reader to do. No design in this category needs a behaviour — no disclosure, no tabs, no filter, no slider, no timer, and no hover state on a number. The controls pass adds one opt-in module, count-up, off by default and edit-safe, so every frame here is still a resting state and §7.7's “behaviour states” has one value to draw rather than none.

That makes the real work typographic and arithmetic. A stat is two lines of text with a strict relationship, and a section is two, three or four of them holding one baseline while their labels run to three lines underneath. The four things §8 asks A10 to settle are all consequences of that: how many fit in a row, what a unit does to a value's width, where a source line goes, and what a cell looks like when the label is missing or sixty characters long.

The refusal that defined the category was the count-up, and this pass overturns it. A number that animates from zero when it scrolls into view was refused in all fifteen designs at every motion setting, on the argument that the figure is not there until a script puts it there. count-up is in the fixed registry and its own degradation reads “The final value renders as static text — it is already in the HTML before JS ever runs.” So the module ships as an opt-in section toggle, Off by default, and the reasons — with the one that survives, on 9 Bars' fill — are on the frame below.

The value is a text field, not a number field. 41,800, 1.2, 99.98 and Sold out are all things a site types into that box, and a numeric field with a formatter would have to guess the separator, the decimal places and the locale and would be wrong somewhere. Twelve characters, no formatting applied, drawn as typed. ⚑ Mine. Prefix and suffix are separate fields drawn at 0.5em of the value, in text and not muted, sitting on the value's own baseline — not superscript, not raised, not lifted to the cap line. A muted unit reads as a caption stuck to a number; a raised one reads as a footnote marker. Half the value's size is what keeps “£” from competing with “82,400” while staying the same colour, because it is part of the same word. ⚑ Mine. Spacing has two cases and no third. %, ×, °, +, − and any currency symbol are set tight. Anything containing a letter takes a 0.16em space — 3.4 hrs, 40 /mo — except a single capital used as a scale abbreviation, K, M, B, which is set tight because it is part of the figure. The value and its units never wrap apart at any width: they are one inline run with no break opportunity inside it. ⚑ Mine. A stat with no unit reads as the bare value at full size and nothing is substituted. No “#”, no “units”, no inferred “+”, no thousands abbreviation, no invented word in the label's place. Nine of the eleven stats in this category's content have no unit, which makes the bare value the ordinary case rather than the deficient one. Tabular figures everywhere, in every design, at every size. A column of values that shifts by a few pixels when a 1 becomes a 4 is the defect this prevents, and it costs nothing in a value that stands alone. ⚑ Mine.

CELLS TOP-ALIGNED · THREE VALUES ON ONE BASELINE · THE HOLE FALLS AT THE FOOT OF THE MIDDLE CELL
The value comes first in the cell in fourteen of the fifteen designs, and this is why. With the value first and the cells top-aligned, every value in a row shares one baseline whatever its label does — a missing label leaves a hole at the foot of a cell, where nothing was going to be read, and a three-line label pushes nothing. Put the label first and a missing one lifts its value 22 px above its neighbours', which reads as a mistake rather than an absence. ⚑ Mine. A stat with no label is drawn as the value alone and nothing is invented — no em dash, no “—”, no repeated unit, no “Total”. A stat with no value is not saved, so there is no reverse case: value is the one required field in the repeater. 60 characters is the label's ceiling, and labels wrap. They are never truncated, never clamped to a line count, never ellipsised and never shrunk to fit. At the narrowest cell in the category — 306 px at four across — 60 characters is three lines at 15 px, which is the height the ugly test was run against. The editor's counter turns muted past 34 characters with “Shorter labels line up better across a row”: advice, not a limit. ⚑ Mine. Labels are sentence case, 15 px, in text-muted, and never uppercase. An uppercase tracked label under a 40 px numeral is a second eyebrow, and the section already has one. 15 px is §2's floor and the label sits on it in every design and at every width — the label is the only text in A10 that never steps. One design puts the label above the value, as a control value rather than a default: 1 Row's Order — Value first · Label above. At Label above the label block is equalised to the tallest label in the row so the values still share a baseline. It is the only equalisation of a text block in A10 — 2 Cards equalises card height, which is a container and not a stat — and it is stated on the frame. ⚑ Mine.
THE FIFTH SETTLEMENT NOBODY ASKED FOR · THE COUNT-UP, REFUSED IN ALL FIFTEEN A number that animates from zero when it scrolls into view is refused in every design at every motion setting, and it is the only refusal in A10 with four separate reasons. It animates the content itself, so reduced motion cannot honour it without changing what the section says — every other behaviour in this library can be made instant and still be the same section. It is unreadable while it runs: the one moment a reader looks at the number is the moment it is not the number. It re-runs, because scroll-into-view fires again on the way back up. And it lies to assistive technology, which reads whatever figure is in the DOM at the moment it gets there. So A10 has one module and no other motion: count-up at Count up On, and at the default Off no behaviour in any of the fifteen — no timer, no transition, and no hover state on a stat at any setting. A number is not interactive, and a hover plane under something that cannot be pressed is an invitation to press it. The only hover and focus states in the category belong to two links — the optional section link and the source line when a URL is authored — both of which are A6's treatment unchanged. §7.7 asks for a behaviour-states frame per design; A10 draws a states frame instead, showing the link's hover and focus and the design's missing-field cases, and every design's panel now says what it declares: count-up, off by default, and nothing else. ⚑ Mine, and the category's headline finding — amended by the controls pass rather than withdrawn: nothing in A10 needs JavaScript, and one thing in it may now use some. Four other things a stats section reaches for, refused with reasons. A sparkline, dial, donut or pie beside a value — a chart is a different kind of object with axes, legends and a data series, and 9 Bars' single proportional bar is as far as a section goes. An icon per stat — refused on a maintenance premise that the curated Icon Picker removed, and reinstated in the controls pass. The sixty-icons-to-maintain argument predates P0·2: the picker is one curated set, so Icons None · Shown ships as a section toggle on fourteen designs, off by default, with the slot above the value. 11 Inline is the exception — there is no position for a glyph inside a sentence — and “a briefcase above 340 issues sent tells a reader nothing the label did not” survives as advice rather than as a refusal. Colour as meaning: no green up, no red down, because the packs supply one accent and no semantic pair, and 14 Change carries direction in a glyph and a word instead. A live figure pulled from an API — Ghost holds no such number, and a section that fetches is a section that can be empty at load.

STRUCTURE, SPACE, ACCENT, RESPONSIVE, PRINT Structure. <section aria-labelledby> named by its title, an <h2>. The set is a <ul> of <li>, and each stat is a <p> holding the value and the label — one paragraph, so a screen reader announces “41,800, readers on the Thursday letter” as one string and not as two unrelated fragments. The value is not a heading in any design, at any size: fifteen headings that are all numerals is a heading rotor full of digits. Three structures were refused: <dl><dt><dd>, because a label is not a term and a value is not its definition and support for the pattern is still uneven; <table>, which 3 Grid and 8 Ledger most tempt, because there is no header row and nothing compares down a column; and <data value>, which adds a machine-readable figure nothing in the theme reads. ⚑ Mine, all three. Four things to notice, and the first is why this category is harder to tokenise than A9. The numbers change typeface. Georgia's 41,800 and Bricolage's 41,800 are not the same width, the same weight or the same voice — Georgia's figures have serifs and a lower contrast at 40 px, Bricolage's are close-set and heavy — and the row holds anyway because the cells are fixed at 416 and the value is the first thing in each of them. A design that centred its values, or sized a cell to its content, would move on a re-skin.

WHAT THE STRESS FRAME FORCED The value ladder had to stop at Large 52 at four across. £1,284,900 p a is twelve characters plus two units, which is 296 px at Row 40 in Georgia and would be 500 px at Display 68 — wider than the 306 cell, wrapping mid-figure, which is the one thing a value must never do. Display 68 is therefore not offered at four across in any design, and the control says “Large at four across”. This is the amendment the stress frame forced on the whole category. The 60-character label is three lines at 306 px and it pushes nothing, because the values are on one baseline above it and the cells are top-aligned. The labelless third cell is a 40 px tall cell beside three 80 px ones, and it reads as a number that needs no explaining rather than as a missing field. The row's own height is the tallest cell's, and no cell is stretched to match it. Two units on one value is legal and ugly, and it is the site's ugly. A prefix and a suffix both authored draws both, tight and spaced by their own rules; the editor does not warn, because £4.20 /mo is a reasonable thing to want. “Sold out” as a value proves the field is text — set in the heading font at the value's size, it reads as a figure in a row of figures, which is what a site that types it wants. The 104-character title at Medium 34 is three lines on a 780 measure, and it is the reason the title ladder stops there: at Large 40 the same string is four lines and the section's head is taller than its content.
