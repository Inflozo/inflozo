Poster

One featured post in a narrow centred column: a tall portrait crop at the top, then the eyebrow, title, excerpt, author and call to action beneath it. A magazine cover rather than a web hero — the picture is the object and the type is its caption, in that order and at that weight.

It is the only design in A19 that defaults to Portrait 4:5 and the only one whose default composition is centred. Both follow from the column: a 560 px measure has no room for a second element beside anything, so the section stops trying to fill the page's width and spends its height instead. Like 2 Full Bleed it cannot exist without a picture and hands off to 1 Split.

The column is centred rather than flush because a narrow flush-left column at 1440 reads as a layout that failed to fill, and Alignment offers Flush left for the sites that want that anyway.
The eyebrow carries two words joined by a middle dot — the section's “Featured” and the post's tag. ⚑ It is the only design in A19 that combines them, and it exists because a centred column has one line for a label and a poster wants to say what kind of piece it is. If the post has no tag the eyebrow is “Featured” alone; if the section's eyebrow is cleared it is the tag alone; if both are absent the line is not drawn.
28 px between the picture and the type block, and the type block's own rhythm is 13. At 700 px of picture the gap has to be large enough to read as a caption's distance rather than as a crop's edge, and small enough that the two stay one object.

ALIGNMENT FLUSH LEFT · THE COLUMN MOVES TO THE PAGE MARGIN
Width is the column, not the picture: Narrow 560 · Medium 620 · Wide 720, and the picture is always the column's full width. Ratio sets the height from there — at Narrow, Portrait is 700, Square is 560, Landscape is 373. Wide 16:9 is not offered: at 720 wide it is 405 tall, which is a banner in a column and 1 Split does that better with the space it has. Alignment Flush left moves the whole column to the page margin, type and picture together. The type does not stay centred inside a left-hung column and the picture does not stay put — one value, one movement. ⚑ Flush left plus Wide 720 is the closest this design comes to 1 Split and the panel names the overlap rather than preventing it. The measure is the column at every width, so a 34 px title at Narrow runs about 34 characters a line. That is short, and it is why Title size is not a control here — 34 is the only size that works across all three widths, and a poster that lets its title grow to 40 at 560 wraps every title in the roster to four lines.

WHAT THIS PANEL SETTLES Width takes Title size's slot, and the trade is deliberate. Every other picture design in A19 lets a user set the title's size against a fixed column; here the column is the variable and the title is fixed at 34. One of the two has to be constant or the design has no proportion left, and a poster's proportion is its column. Meta With photograph is the default here rather than an option — the second of the three designs that spend profile_image. A centred column has an axis and a 24 px circle sits on it; in a left-hung meta row it would be one more thing at the left edge. Post 5's author has no photograph and draws initials, A1·6's fallback. Cut: a Media side control — there are no sides in a single column. Cut: a caption field under the picture. Ghost has no per-post caption for a feature image and inventing one would put a field in A19's union that no other category could fill; a finding for the architect if A25 needs one for Koenig images.

Four token swaps and nothing else, so this frame is drawn at a second setting rather than repeating the primary one — Width Medium, Square, Flush left — which is also the answer to “does dark change the structure”. It does not, and per the brief the frame would be optional; it is drawn because a 620 px square of photograph is the largest continuous image area in A19 outside 2 Full Bleed, and it is worth seeing that it needs no dimming the way a full-bleed picture does. The difference is the ground around it: a picture with page on all four sides reads at full strength in dark; a picture that meets the section's edges does not.
