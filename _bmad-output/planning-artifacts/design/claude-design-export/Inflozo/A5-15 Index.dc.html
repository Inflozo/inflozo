A large numeral where the icon would be. It is the editorial answer to the media question — a set of features counted rather than illustrated, for a site with no photographs and no wish to choose six glyphs. Its one real obligation is the boundary it has to hold: a number implies an order, and this design is an index rather than a sequence. A13 Process owns sequence, and the editor says so when a user's titles start reading as steps.

1 Three Up's grid with the numeral in the media slot: 44 px in the heading font, in text-muted, on a hairline that runs the item's full width, with the item's meta at the rule's other end. That row — numeral, rule, meta — is the design, and it is why this is the only grid in A5 whose items have an internal division. The numeral is larger than the item's title by half again, which is what keeps it a mark rather than a heading; muted is the default because a 44 px accent numeral six times over is the accent spent six times. Row gap 44 rather than 40, since the rule at the top of each item needs air above it. Flagged as mine: the 44 px numeral, its muted colour, the rule-and-meta row, and the 44 px row gap.

Letters exist because of the boundary. A site whose six features are genuinely unordered can label them A to F and no reader will look for a first step; the editor offers the value in exactly those words when it notices ordinal language in the titles. Numerals are padded at the 01 value so that 01 and 12 occupy the same width and the rules under them start at the same place — the alternative is a grid whose hairlines begin at two different x positions. Accent is the colour for a set of three or four, where the accent is spent three times rather than six, and the editor says so rather than disabling it. Flagged as mine: all three numeral values, the padding rule, and the accent advice.

THE BOUNDARY WITH A13 PROCESS
A number in front of a thing says “first, second, third”, and most sets of features are not. This design's numerals count the items so a reader can refer to one — an index — and nothing joins one item to the next: no connector, no line between the numerals, no arrow, and no state. A13 Process owns sequence: steps that happen in an order, with whatever connects them and whatever says where a reader is in them. It is a different category with different structure, and a site that wants “Step 1 · Step 2” should be there. The editor's part in it: where the item titles begin with ordinal or sequential words — First, Then, Next, Finally, Step — it names A13 Process and offers the Letters value as the alternative. It advises and does not prevent, since a site may genuinely have an ordered feature set and be right to draw it here. Flagged as mine: the whole boundary, the refusal of connectors, and the editor's ordinal-language check.

1 · The numeral is aria-hidden. It is generated from the item's position, not authored, and a screen reader already announces “1 of 6” for a list item — hearing “01” before every title is that same fact twice. This is why the list is a <ul> rather than an <ol>: an ordered list says the order matters, and the whole design says it does not. 2 · A5-0's structure otherwise unchanged: the section named by its h2, items an <li> each with an h3. The numeral is not part of the heading — a heading that begins “01” is a heading whose first word is a number in every search result and every outline. 3 · The meta is plain text, third in the DOM after the title and body whatever its visual position — 6 Rows' rule, carried. At Rule Hide it moves under the body visually as well, which brings the two into agreement. The rule is a border-bottom on the numeral's row, 1.2:1 light and 1.3:1 dark, decorative. Nothing depends on seeing it: at Rule Hide the same information is in the same order. Contrast · numeral muted 5.4:1 light / 6.4:1 dark, accent 4.8:1 / 6.1:1 — large text either way · title 12.1:1 / 12.6:1 · body 5.4:1 / 6.4:1 · meta 5.4:1 / 6.4:1. Nothing is interactive on this design unless the section action is shown: there is no item link, by decision.
