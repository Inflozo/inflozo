Thumb Side

A square thumbnail at the left, title and meta at the right, two columns of rows. The only design in A17 with media at the left — every other image-bearing design puts the photograph above the text, and that one difference changes what the design is for.

A thumbnail beside a title is a picture doing identification rather than invitation. It is not there to make a reader want the post; it is there to help them recognise which post it is. That is why the image is small, square and never larger than a quarter of the row — and why this is the right design for “twenty posts and photographs that are not very good”.

It is also the one design in the category whose row survives a phone unchanged. The category floor collapses thirteen designs to one column at 767 because a 163 px feature image is a thumbnail; this design's image already is one, so it has nothing to give up. Two other image-bearing designs keep two columns — 14 Dense by dropping to a title under a very small picture, and 18 Edge to Edge by dividing the screen in two with no margin to spend.

Eight rows in two 636 columns: a 120 px square thumb, a 20 px gap, and a 496 px text column. The thumb is flex: none and the text takes the remainder, so a long title lengthens its own row and the thumb never shrinks — the opposite of the usual failure, where a thumbnail is squeezed by its neighbour's text.
Square 1:1 is the only ratio, and it is not a control. A 4:5 thumb beside two lines of text leaves the text floating; a 16:9 thumb 120 px tall is 213 px wide and stops being a thumbnail. Square is the only ratio that reads as identification at every step of the ladder — which makes this the one image-bearing design in A17 with no Image ratio control. ⚑ Mine.
Rows are separated by 32 px of space and no hairline. 10 Ledger rules its rows because it has nothing else to mark them; here the thumb's left edge does it — eight squares down a column are a stronger rhythm than eight lines. Both together was drawn and cut: a rule plus a thumb reads as a table of contents in a manual.
Post 3's plate is a 120 px square with its tag at 11 px, one step below the eyebrow's 13 — the only place in A17 where the plate's type is not eyebrow size, because a tracked 13 px word does not fit a 120 px box. It is the smallest plate in the category and the least conspicuous, which is a real advantage of a small image: a missing photograph costs this row almost nothing.

At twenty posts this is the only design in the category that keeps its photographs. 14 Dense fits twenty in 196 px cells; 10 Ledger fits them as text. This one fits twenty rows at 96 px and every post still has its picture — because a thumbnail's cost is a fixed 96 px whatever the row's width, where a feature image's cost grows with the column. That is the design's real argument: it is the only A17 design where the image is cheap. Everywhere else the image is the row's largest object and the layout is arranged around it; here it is the smallest object and the title leads. Which inverts the purpose — scanning a known archive rather than being drawn into an unknown one — and is why the thumb never passes a quarter of the row. ⚑ It is also the design most likely to be used badly, and I have not prevented it. A site with beautiful photography that picks this design has thrown the photography away, and nothing in the panel says so. The picker's note describes what it is — “A small square thumbnail beside each title” — rather than judging the user's pictures. A note reading “use this if your images are weak” would be insulting and unreliable. The design is offered plainly and the choice is the site's.
