Inflozo · Architecture

How Inflozo works

Written for a human, not an engineer. No jargon that isn't explained in the same sentence. Every number here was measured on real hardware, not estimated.

1

What it is

Inflozo is a visual site builder for Ghost — the blogging and newsletter platform. Someone who runs a Ghost site drags ready-made sections onto a page, adjusts them, and presses Deploy. Inflozo turns that into a real Ghost theme and installs it on their site.

A theme is the set of files that decides how a Ghost site looks. Normally you write them by hand, in code. Inflozo writes them for you.

484 designs

Ready-made sections across 34 categories — headers, heroes, post grids, footers. 70 are free.

Their site, not ours

The finished website lives entirely on the customer's own Ghost. Inflozo is not in the path when a visitor loads it.

2

The one idea everything rests on

If you remember one thing from this document, make it this one.

A site builder makes a promise: what you see while designing is what actually ships. That promise is easy to make and very hard to keep, because most builders draw the preview with one piece of code and build the real output with another. Two pieces of code, written at different times by different people, slowly drift apart. The preview starts lying.

Inflozo avoids this by having one description of every section, read by two renderers that share almost all of their code. The preview and the shipped theme aren't kept in sync by discipline — they're the same machinery pointed at two outputs.

The only real difference: the canvas fills in the actual values so you can see your page, while the theme leaves labelled blanks for Ghost to fill in when a visitor arrives.

This is now proven, not just intended. A test renders one section through both paths and compares the results piece by piece — every element, every style name, every attribute must match. It runs on every change. Until recently only half of this existed, so the promise was a claim nothing could disprove. It is now a claim that fails loudly if anyone breaks it.

3

The pieces, and how they fit

Five services. Only one of them is Inflozo.

Inflozo is a workshop, not a host. It builds the thing, hands it over, and steps away. That is a deliberate design choice and it shapes almost every other decision here.

Why that matters commercially. Inflozo never pays to serve anyone's website traffic, never becomes a bottleneck, and never takes a customer's site down by having a bad day. It also means an outage at Inflozo is an inconvenience for people who are mid-design — not an emergency for people whose business runs on their site.

4

What happens when you press Deploy

Five steps. Every timing below was measured against a real Ghost site.

The goal was "first site live within ten minutes". The machine part takes thirteen seconds — the remaining time is entirely the human deciding what they want.

The check step is the interesting one

Ghost has its own theme validator, and it is strict — it will refuse a theme outright rather than install something broken. Inflozo runs that exact validator before uploading, plus its own additional checks for things Ghost doesn't look at (accessibility, colour contrast, valid HTML, leftover scaffolding).

The heavy test theme currently passes both Ghost versions with zero errors and zero warnings. Not "a few harmless warnings" — zero. That is the standard being held to.

When it goes wrong

Deliberately broken deploys were tested against a live site: a corrupted file, a file that isn't a theme, switching on a theme that doesn't exist, and a theme with a fatal error inside. All four were refused cleanly with a specific message, and in every case the customer's live site was left completely untouched. Nothing was half-applied.

Whose data is this, and what happens if we lose it

This is the question worth being precise about, because it decides how much backup effort is justified.

Everything Inflozo stores is the customer's — not internal company records. But the stakes vary enormously between one kind and another, and it's worth splitting them.

The left column is why an Inflozo outage is never an emergency. The red box on the right is why backups are not optional.

So: is it customer data or internal data?

Customer data, almost entirely. The only genuinely internal records are billing history and the community suggestions board — and the billing records also exist at the payment provider, so they are not the risk.

The risk is concentrated in three things, in order of severity:

WhatIf it's lost
Their original theme
taken automatically before Inflozo first changes their site		 They can never put their site back the way it was. There is no other copy anywhere in the world. This is the "undo button" for having tried Inflozo at all. unrecoverable
The pages they designed Their live site keeps working, but every hour they spent designing is gone and they start again from a blank canvas. A customer would very reasonably leave over this. rebuildable, painfully
Their uploaded images Possibly their only copy, depending on whether they kept the originals. maybe unrecoverable

The real danger isn't a disaster — it's an ordinary bug. Hosting providers don't lose files; that essentially doesn't happen any more. What does happen is that a piece of housekeeping code deletes the wrong thing. Inflozo will have five different places in its code that are allowed to delete files, spread across four separate pieces of work. That's five chances for a mistake, and right now there is no undo behind any of them.

The database has an undo — a "rewind to before the mistake" feature. The files have nothing. That gap is the open question, and it is deliberately still open rather than quietly decided.

What was found when this was actually tested

The plan has always said "practise restoring from a backup before launch". That practice run had never been done. It was done, and the backup did not work — it produced a database that looked perfectly restored and had quietly lost the link between every piece of work and the person who owned it. Everything was there, belonging to nobody.

Both problems are now fixed and written up as a step-by-step procedure. The thing that caught them was running the security tests against the restored copy — nothing about the restore itself looked wrong.

6

The rules that keep it safe

There are 36 written rules the whole system is built from. These are the ones that matter most, in plain terms.

The database enforces this itself. It isn't a rule the code has to remember — the browser is refused at the door even if the app asks wrongly.

Nobody sees anyone else's work

Enforced by the database, not by the app. Tested by having one account try to read, edit and delete another's — and fail every time.

The browser never holds the Ghost keys

They're encrypted, and only one small piece of server code can ever unlock them. Verified: a leaked key can't reach them.

Nothing a user types can become code

If someone types something that looks like an instruction into a heading, it ships as visible text, never as something that runs.

Facts the user shouldn't set, they can't set

Their storage usage, their plan, their deploy history — the server asserts those. The browser is refused, on creating and editing.

The honest part. Connecting a Ghost site means handing Inflozo a key that can read that site's subscriber email addresses and publish posts on it. Ghost offers no narrower key — that's a Ghost limitation, not a choice. Inflozo's own code only ever uses it for four specific things, and every use will be logged. But the key itself is powerful, and the connect screen should say so plainly rather than let people find out later.

7

What we know because we measured it

Four rounds of deliberately trying to break this, all against real systems — two real Ghost sites, a real database, real payments.

13 sec

A full deploy, start to finish, on a deliberately oversized page. The target was ten minutes.

0 errors

Ghost's own theme validator, on both supported Ghost versions. Zero warnings too.

67 checks

Security tests that run against the database. All passing — and proven to actually fail when something breaks.

1.4 KB

The stylesheet a visitor downloads, against a 40 KB budget. Comfortable, though this figure needs rechecking on real designs.

The most valuable thing found

The security test suite — the thing that had been reporting "all clear" for three rounds — could not actually fail. It printed the word FAIL and then reported success anyway, so anything watching it saw a green tick. Six real problems had been hiding behind it.

It's now a genuine gate, and that was verified the only honest way: by deliberately breaking nine things one at a time and confirming it caught all nine.

That finding is also the reason for a habit that now runs through everything here: a test that has never been seen to fail is not yet a test. The same logic applied to the backup — which is exactly why the restore practice found two defects the moment it was finally run.
