What it is

Inflozo is a visual site builder for Ghost — the publishing platform people use for blogs and paid newsletters. Someone who runs a Ghost site opens Inflozo, drags ready-made sections onto a page, adjusts them until it looks right, and presses Deploy. Inflozo turns that into a real Ghost theme and installs it on their site.

A theme is the bundle of files that decides how a Ghost site looks — its layout, its typography, its colours. Normally you write those files by hand, in code. That's the wall most Ghost owners hit: they can write beautifully and they cannot write templates. Inflozo writes the files for them.

484 designs

Across 34 categories — headers, heroes, post grids, pricing tables, footers. 70 are free.

Two Ghost versions

Ghost 5 and Ghost 6 are both supported, and they genuinely differ. Both are tested against, every time.

Their site, not ours

The finished website runs entirely on the customer's own Ghost. Inflozo isn't involved when a visitor loads it.

Nothing to learn

No code, no template language, no build step. Drag, adjust, deploy.

2

The one idea it rests on

If you remember one thing from this document, make it this.

Every site builder makes the same promise: what you see while designing is what actually ships. It's an easy promise to make and a very hard one to keep, because almost every builder draws the preview with one piece of code and produces the real output with another. Two pieces of code, written at different times, drifting slowly apart. Eventually the preview lies — and the customer only finds out after they've published.

Inflozo avoids that by having one description of each section, read by two renderers that share nearly all of their code. The preview and the shipped theme aren't kept in step by anyone's discipline. They're the same machinery, pointed at two different outputs.

The only real difference: the canvas fills in your actual content so you can see your page, while the theme leaves labelled blanks for Ghost to fill in when a visitor arrives.

Why that's more than a nice idea

The two outputs have to differ in exactly two ways, and be identical in every other. A list of posts expands into real posts on the canvas, but becomes a repeat instruction in the theme. A title resolves to real text on the canvas, but becomes a placeholder in the theme. Everything else — the structure, the style names, every setting — must match precisely, because the stylesheet that makes it look right selects on those.

This is now proven rather than intended. A test renders one section through both paths and compares them piece by piece — every element, every style name, every setting. It also checks that the two intended differences are present, so nobody can "fix" them into matching. Until recently only one of the two renderers existed, which means the product's central promise was a claim nothing could disprove. It now fails loudly if anyone breaks it.

3

The pieces

Five services. Only one of them is Inflozo, and it steps out of the way once the work is done.

Inflozo is a workshop, not a host. It builds the thing, hands it over, and walks away. That single choice shapes almost every other decision in this document. Two further services sit behind Inflozo and never touch a customer's site — Dodo for payments and Resend for email — so they are left out of the picture rather than cluttering it.

Why that matters commercially. Inflozo never pays to serve anybody's website traffic, never becomes a bottleneck as customers grow, and can never take a customer's site down by having a bad day. An outage is an inconvenience for people mid-design — not an emergency for people whose business runs on their site.

Two doors into Ghost, and they never swap

Ghost offers two ways in, and Inflozo uses each for exactly one thing.

DoorUsed forWho holds the key
The reading door Fetching posts and images to show on your canvas while you design. The browser, directly. This key is designed to be public — it's the same one any Ghost theme uses.
The writing door Installing the theme. Four operations only: upload a theme, switch it on, upload the routes file, and clear the announcement bar if you ask. The server, never the browser. One small piece of code is the only thing in the entire system that can unlock this key.

The honest part. Ghost has no such thing as a limited key. The one that installs your theme can also read your subscribers' email addresses and publish posts on your site. That's a Ghost limitation, not a choice — verified on both Ghost versions. Inflozo's own code only ever uses it for those four things, and every use gets logged. But the key itself is powerful, and the screen that asks for it should say so plainly rather than let people find out later.

A section, end to end

Follow one piece of one page from the design team's hands to a visitor's screen.

One description, authored once, used five different ways. Nobody re-writes the section for the preview and again for the output.

What a "setting" actually is

When a customer changes a section — wider, darker, centred — Inflozo doesn't generate new code. It writes a single label onto that section, and the section's stylesheet already knows what that label means. One mechanism, used by all 484 designs.

This sounds like an implementation detail and is actually the reason the library can be that large. If every design invented its own way of being adjusted, 484 designs would be 484 separate problems. Because they share one mechanism, a setting behaves identically everywhere, and the preview and the shipped page can be compared automatically.

Pressing Deploy

Five steps. Every timing below was measured against a real Ghost site, on a deliberately oversized page.

The goal is "first site live within ten minutes". The machine part takes thirteen seconds — the rest is the human deciding what they want.

The check step is the interesting one

Ghost ships its own theme validator, and it's strict — it will refuse a theme outright rather than install something broken. Inflozo runs that exact validator before uploading, plus its own extra checks for things Ghost doesn't look at: accessibility, colour contrast, valid markup, and leftover scaffolding that should never reach a finished file.

The heavy test theme passes both Ghost versions with zero errors and zero warnings. Not "a few harmless warnings" — zero. That's the standard being held to, and it's checked on every build.

A subtlety that cost a round to find. Ghost 5 and Ghost 6 each bundle a different version of that validator, and their rules genuinely differ. Running the newer one twice with a "check as if version 5" flag looks equivalent and isn't — on the same theme it reported zero problems while real Ghost 5 reported one. Inflozo now runs each Ghost's own validator version, so the answer matches what the customer will actually see.

Where the data lives

Worth being precise about, because it decides what needs protecting.

The left column is why an Inflozo outage is never an emergency. The red box on the right is why the customer is asked to take their own backup before anything changes.

Whose data is it?

The customer's, almost entirely. The only genuinely internal records are billing history and the public suggestions board — and billing also exists at the payment provider, so it isn't the risk. Everything else belongs to the person who made it.

If this were lostWhat happens
Their original theme They can never put their site back the way it was. No other copy exists anywhere. This is the undo button for having tried Inflozo at all. unrecoverable
The pages they designed Their live site keeps working, but every hour of design is gone and they start from a blank canvas. A customer would reasonably leave over this. rebuildable, painfully
Their uploaded images Less serious than it looks. Once deployed, images are packed inside the theme on the customer's own Ghost, so they exist twice. Only not-yet-deployed images live solely in Inflozo. mostly safe

Decided: Inflozo is not a backup tool and does not try to be. It keeps no copy of the customer's Ghost data. Instead, before the first deploy to a site, the customer is blocked until they confirm they hold their own backup — with a checklist showing where each thing lives in Ghost and exactly how to save it.

The gate states what Inflozo changes — the theme, and the routes file — and then recommends a full backup anyway. Accurate first, because a warning that exaggerates gets clicked through, and a gate everyone clicks through isn't a gate.

Two facts from Ghost's own documentation that the checklist must be honest about, because both are easy to get wrong and one is genuinely dangerous:

  • Ghost's content export does not include images. People export, see a file, and assume they're covered. They aren't. This is the single most important line on the list.
  • Ghost's own hosting customers cannot download their images at all. No export exists and there's no server to copy from. Inflozo can't fix that, so the gate says so instead of implying they're safe.

Being straight about what a consent gate is: it moves responsibility, it doesn't reduce risk. Someone who ticks the box without actually backing up still loses their old theme. That's a deliberate trade — the same one every tool of this kind makes — recorded as a decision rather than left to be discovered.

Who is allowed to do what

Enforced by the database itself, not by the application remembering to check.

The browser is refused at the door even if the application asks wrongly. That's the difference between a rule and a control.

Nobody sees anyone else's work

Tested by having one account try to read, edit and delete another's — refused every time, including the subtler routes like counting rows to learn they exist.

The browser never holds Ghost keys

Encrypted, and only one small piece of server code can unlock them. Verified: even a leaked master key cannot reach the decrypted values.

Typed text can never become code

If someone types something that looks like an instruction into a heading, it ships as visible text. Proven against every variation tried.

Facts users shouldn't set, they can't

Storage used, plan, deploy history, moderation status. Refused on creating and editing — an early version only blocked editing, which handed it all back.

The rule behind the rules

Four separate security problems found in testing turned out to be one idea missing. In each, a value from outside was carefully escaped — special characters made harmless — and then handed to something that interprets it.

Escaping is the wrong tool there. A web link reading javascript:… contains no special character to escape, so it passes through untouched and then runs when someone clicks. The fix isn't four patches; it's a rule: parse the value and rebuild it from validated parts — never paste it into something that will interpret it. That rule is now written down, built, and tested on both renderers.

8

Editing safely

What happens to work in progress, and what happens when two devices collide.

Local-first: your work lives in your browser as you type and is copied up periodically, so a dropped connection costs nothing.

The takeover rule is a good example of a product decision and a security fix being the same thing. For the displaced device to be told it was taken over, it has to be able to detect it — and detection works off a counter that must increase on every takeover. Testing found the counter could be skipped, meaning both devices would believe they held the session and the warning could never appear. The database now refuses any takeover that doesn't advance it. One rule, both problems.

When things go wrong

Deliberately broken deploys, run against a real Ghost site. Four behaved. One didn't.

What was done on purposeWhat happened
Uploaded a corrupted fileRefused with a clear message. Site untouched.clean
Uploaded a valid file that isn't a themeRefused. Site untouched.clean
Switched on a theme that doesn't existRefused. Site untouched.clean
Uploaded a theme with a fatal error insideRefused outright by Ghost. Site untouched.clean
Two deploys of the same theme at once One succeeded; the other crashed and leaked a piece of the server's folder structure in the error message.see below

Nothing broke the site. After all five, both test sites were serving pages normally. No failure ever left a half-applied theme, which is the promise that matters most — and it had never actually been checked before.

That fifth one is reassurance more than alarm. Ghost has no protection against two simultaneous deploys — but Inflozo already puts a lock around the operation for exactly this reason. That was previously an argument on paper; now it's a demonstrated failure that the lock prevents. The remaining work is making sure that crash message never reaches a customer, since it contains part of their server's directory layout.

Rolling back

Every deploy keeps a copy of exactly what was installed, so going back is replaying a stored file rather than rebuilding. That distinction matters: rebuilding an old design against today's section library would produce a different theme, because the library keeps improving.

The last 10 versions are kept on the paid plan and 3 on free, with the option to pin ones worth keeping. Because those files can't be regenerated, that limit is the real boundary on how far back someone can go — so the interface says so plainly rather than showing a history list with buttons that don't work.

The rules, in brief

There are 36 written rules the whole system is built from. These are the ones a non-engineer would actually want to know, in plain terms.

The preview and the shipped page share their code

AD-1 · AD-14

Not "we keep them in sync" — literally the same functions. And the compiler produces byte-for-byte identical output from identical input, on any machine, every time.

The section library is data, not programs

AD-2

A design is a folder of plain files. Nothing imports it; both renderers read it. Deleting the folder deletes the design completely.

Every table is scoped to its owner, the same way

AD-6

One pattern, applied everywhere, so a reviewer checks one shape rather than thirty. Testing later added a second half: owning a piece of work doesn't mean you own the project it hangs from — that had to be checked separately.

The customer never sets a fact the server should

AD-8 · AD-31

Storage used, plan, deploy history. Enforced by the database on creating and editing both, because an earlier version locked only editing and handed everything back.

Anything about Ghost must be proven, not assumed

AD-23

Four confident claims about Ghost made it into the plan and were later shown false — each one had damaged a working feature. Now: cite it or run it, or it doesn't go in.

Untrusted values are parsed, never pasted

AD-36

The rule behind four separate security findings. Escaping protects against odd characters; it does nothing about a value whose meaning is dangerous.

A finding isn't finished until it reaches a rule

AD-36b

Evidence is where something is proven, never where it lives. Three audits found the same failure — big findings get written up at length and never turned into a rule — so a script now checks it and fails the build.

What we measured

Four rounds of deliberately trying to break this, plus a reliability round — all against real systems: two real Ghost sites, a real database, real payments.

13 sec

A full deploy on an oversized page. The target was ten minutes.

0 errors

Ghost's own validator, both versions. Zero warnings too.

70 checks

Security tests on the database. All passing — and proven to fail when something breaks.

1.4 KB

Stylesheet a visitor downloads, against a 40 KB budget. Needs rechecking on real designs.

14×

Speed difference a single missing database index makes at realistic scale. Now added everywhere and checked automatically.

0.7 sec

Delay on the first action after an idle period. Immaterial to every budget; worth knowing for how the first click feels.

The two most valuable things found

The security test suite couldn't fail. The thing reporting "all clear" for three rounds printed the word FAIL and then reported success anyway, so anything watching saw a green tick. Six real problems were hiding behind it.

Now a genuine gate — verified the only honest way, by deliberately breaking nine things one at a time and confirming it caught all nine.

The backup didn't work. The plan had always said "practise restoring before launch". Nobody had. When it was finally done, the restore produced a database that looked perfect and had quietly lost the link between every piece of work and the person who owned it — everything present, belonging to nobody.

Both defects are fixed and written up as a procedure. What caught them was running the security tests against the restored copy — nothing about the restore itself looked wrong.

Those two share a lesson that now runs through everything here: a test that has never been seen to fail is not yet a test, and a backup nobody has restored from is a hypothesis. A later audit found the same disease one level up — checks written to guard a whole category of mistake, which then listed the specific items they guarded, so anything added afterwards went unchecked. Within a day, two things had. Those now work off a live list the database generates.
